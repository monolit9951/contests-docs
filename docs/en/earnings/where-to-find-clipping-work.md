The safest work is the work somebody already paid for. It comes from three places: a streamer directly, with the rate settled in DMs; a brand or the agency running a channel, through selection with an application and work samples; or an intermediary platform with a fixed rate - on DareBay that is the open contest board, where you take a brief yourself, with no application, no interview and no portfolio review. Open contests pay **$1.00 to $10.00 per 1000 views**, and nothing open right now pays below that floor. The threshold is **set by each brief**: from that mark a submission enters the maths, while the maximum payout for one submission is whatever the buyer named, up to $500 per clip. They set the rate, the threshold and the cap at contest creation, all three stand on the card before you submit, and the rate and threshold do not change after launch. For a clipper, entry costs nothing.

Every figure on this page comes from the platform's own data (snapshot: 2026-08-23) and counts only open contests - the ones a clipper can take right now.

## Who pays for clips, and out of whose pocket

The question of which streamers pay for clips breaks in practice into three different markets. The money comes out of different pockets, and that is what decides how predictably it reaches you.

**The streamer, out of their own income.** Donations, subscriptions, channel ads - clips are paid for out of the same wallet. That is how most small and mid-sized channels work: the arrangement is personal, the volume swings month to month, the rate is settled in DMs. The upside is that one person makes the decision and you can be in by the evening. The downside is that you are paid by agreement, and that kind of work ends as fast as it started.

**A brand or the agency running the channel.** Here clips are part of a promo campaign, the budget was agreed in advance and it is usually bigger. In exchange, getting in goes through selection: an application, work samples, sometimes a contract and reporting. They pay reliably but on their own cycle - after a reporting period, not after a video. How long that takes is not the streamer's call. What this side looks like from the inside is covered in [how to commission clips for a campaign](/en/for-brands/commission-clips-for-a-campaign).

**An intermediary platform with a fixed rate.** The buyer is still the same streamer or brand, but the money and the counting move to a neutral side. The buyer puts the budget on the platform, names a rate per 1000 views and a cap per submission, and the platform counts views and distributes the budget by rules fixed before the start. That is how DareBay's PPV contests work: in wallet-backed mode the budget is frozen on the platform before you take the brief.

The most reliable payer is the one who parted with the money before the work began. A direct buyer pays by agreement - and that is exactly where the whole risk sits with the clipper. With a brand the risk is different: not a refusal to pay, but the selection at the door and the long wait.

## Three ways to get work: a private deal, a freelance board, an intermediary

How you get the work matters more than the rate itself: it decides who holds the money, who counts the result, and what happens if the two sides disagree.

| What matters | A direct deal in Telegram | A freelance board | An intermediary platform (DareBay) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who holds the money until payout | The buyer. Until they transfer it, the money is nowhere | The board, if the job runs through its escrow deal; outside the deal, the buyer again | The platform: in wallet-backed mode the contest budget is frozen before the start |
| Who counts the views, and how | The buyer, from their own screenshots | Usually the buyer; the board does not count reach | An independent counter reads the published counter on the site - TikTok, YouTube, Instagram or another site, whichever the contest names |
| Where the risk of non-payment sits | Entirely with the clipper: the buyer can vanish, renegotiate after the fact or understate the figures | Inside a deal, a dispute is settled by the board's staff; outside one, the risk is the same as in DMs | The maths run on a formula fixed before the start; no separate consent from the buyer is needed at that step |
| Are followers required | They usually look at the contractor's audience | They look at rating and portfolio | No: payment tracks the reach of one specific submission |
| Is the rate visible in advance | However you agree it, and it can drift in the chat | In the job description, but haggling downwards is normal | The rate, the threshold and the cap stand on the card before you submit; the rate and threshold do not change after launch |

An intermediary has a price of its own. The rules are the same for everyone: you cannot haggle the rate up, and the terms are set by the buyer at contest creation. The calculation is automatic, but paying the money out can be handled by hand, and we promise no deadline for it. In exchange, you never have to prove your own view count or negotiate it after publication.

## Clipping jobs for beginners: where they are actually posted

Openings for clippers are advertised in four places, and each publishes a different amount of the deal up front.

- **Task boards on clipping platforms.** The card is the advert: rate, threshold, cap, deadline and site are printed before you take anything, and nothing needs approving - you submit a clip, not a candidacy.
- **Posts in Discord, Telegram and X.** Most "clipping jobs for beginners" messages live here, and so do the thinnest terms: a rate in one sentence, the rest agreed in DMs, nothing written down about what is counted.
- **Freelance marketplaces.** A vacancy shape: you send a proposal, compete on rating and samples, and agree a fee per order rather than per 1000 views. On Upwork the proposal itself is paid for, in Connects at $0.15 each<!-- source: https://support.upwork.com/hc/en-us/articles/211062898-Understanding-and-using-Connects 2026-09-20 -->.
- **Agency and studio hiring.** Closest to being employed, and the slowest door for a beginner: selection, a test task, sometimes reporting.

Two filters separate paid work from a sales pitch wearing the word "job". First, which way the money travels: an advert charging for "training", a "starter pack", "verification" or a deposit is selling you something, and clipping work bills the buyer, never the clipper. Second, whether the terms can be read before you work - what is counted, from what mark, and what limits one payment. The longer check is in [clipping scams: nine red flags](/en/about/clipping-scams-red-flags).

A fixed-rate brief is not a vacancy either: no post to be appointed to and no salary, only published terms that anyone may take, with the payout following the reach of one video.

## Where to get the source footage

Footage may be attached to the brief - check the contest terms; if there is none, you pick the source yourself.

The option to attach files to a brief exists in the platform, but not every buyer uses it. Live contests more often name a channel, a broadcast or a product, and you find the material publicly. So the first step is always the same: open the card and read the terms before you submit, not after.

If nothing is attached, the source is taken from:

- a recording of a broadcast on the channel named in the brief (a VOD, a replay, a stream archive);
- the author's own videos and shorts, if the brief is about their channel;
- a brand's material about a product, when the contest is a product one.

What is not taken: somebody else's clips and somebody else's edits, recordings from closed venues, and material whose rights holder explicitly forbids reworking. A restriction from the brief always outranks a general rule: if the buyer named a specific broadcast or banned certain scenes, that is a condition of entry. Not sure a source fits - ask in the contest discussion before you assemble the video.

## How to take a brief on the DareBay board

1. Sign up at [DareBay](https://darebay.com) - an account is all you need, no portfolio and no application.
2. Open the contest board and pick a brief on your subject: streamer clips, reviews, challenges, videos to a brand's brief.
3. Read the card in full: the rate per 1000 views, the threshold, the cap per submission, the total budget, the deadline, the publishing site and any attached footage.
4. Edit the video and publish it on your own account on the site the contest named. Counters are read for TikTok, YouTube, Instagram and the other sites named in the brief.
5. Send DareBay the link to the published video - that link is how the work enters the contest. A hashtag is only needed where the brief spells it out, and for an anonymous submission.
6. From there the contest mechanic runs: the view counter is read, the amount is worked out by formula, and an undistributed wallet-backed budget remainder goes back to the buyer.

How the counting is built, what the threshold does and when the money arrives is in [how pay-per-view works](/en/earnings/how-pay-per-view-works).

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need a portfolio or an application to take a brief?

No. The board is open: you pick a contest and submit work, with no buyer approval at the door. No contractor rating, no work samples, no correspondence with the buyer before you start. Everyone in the contest is on the same terms.

### How do I tell there is real money behind a brief?

Read the card before you submit: it states the rate per 1000 views, the threshold, the cap on one submission and the total budget. In wallet-backed mode that budget is frozen on the platform before you take the brief, and the buyer can no longer recall it. The platform charges nothing for taking part.

### Does the buyer attach source footage to the brief?

They can - then the material sits inside the brief itself. But it is not a general rule: most live contests have no files, and then you pick the source yourself within the terms of the brief. Checking that takes a minute on the card, before you sit down to edit.

### Can I clip a streamer who did not create the contest?

The brief describes whose material is wanted. If a brand created the contest and a partner streamer is the face of the video, that is normal and it is written in the terms. Taking somebody else's channel on your own initiative, when the brief is about something else, gets you nowhere: the work will not match the contest terms and will not pass moderation.

### Do I need an intermediary or an agency to take briefs from abroad?

No. There is no manager between you and the brief: the contest is visible on the board, the terms are open, and work is submitted by link. The only intermediary here is the platform itself, and it stands on the side of the calculation, not on the side of the negotiation.

### Are there clipping jobs for beginners with no followers?

Yes, wherever the pay is tied to the reach of one video instead of the size of a channel. An open brief on the board sets no audience floor: the rate is applied to the views that one clip collects, so a new account and a large one are paid by the same formula. A fresh profile does change how a site hands out impressions in the first weeks, but that is a question of reach, not of admission - what it does to the figure is in [how much you can earn from clips](/en/earnings/how-much-clipping-pays).

### Are clipping jobs remote?

By construction, almost always. The work is editing and publishing, so the source, the terms and the finished video all travel as links, and there is nothing to attend: no office, no shift, no time zone to match, only the deadline printed on the card. A direct arrangement with a streamer or an agency can add calls and reporting on somebody else's schedule, but that is a condition of that particular deal rather than of the craft.
